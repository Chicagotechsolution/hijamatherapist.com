The practice

What hijama is — said plainly.

Hijama is a traditional practice in which small cups are applied to the skin to create gentle suction, followed by superficial scratches so that a small amount of blood is drawn into the cups. Many people seek it as part of their cultural or religious tradition, or because they find the sessions relaxing and restorative.

We describe hijama honestly: it is a traditional practice, not a medical treatment. We make no claims that it cures or treats any condition. What we do promise is the practice performed carefully — correct cup placement, a calm room, and hygiene standards you can see for yourself.

Dry cupping (suction only, no incisions) is also offered for those who prefer it.

Not a substitute for medical care

Hijama is not a replacement for seeing a doctor, taking prescribed medication, or seeking diagnosis for any symptom that worries you. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant, or take blood-thinning medication, speak with your physician before booking.

We will decline a session rather than proceed when it isn't appropriate — and we'll tell you why.

Standards

Hygiene you don't have to take on faith.

Single-use everything

Cups, blades, gloves, and drapes are sterile, sealed, and opened in front of you. Nothing that touches skin is ever reused.

Skin preparation and aftercare

The area is cleaned and disinfected before cups are placed, and dressed afterward. You leave with written aftercare instructions, not guesswork.

Safe disposal

Used sharps and cups go directly into a sealed sharps container, handled and disposed of the way a clinic would.

A screening conversation first

Every first session starts with questions about your health, medications, and history. Some situations call for your doctor's go-ahead before we proceed — we'll say so plainly.

What to expect

A first session, from door to door.

Sessions are unhurried — plan for about an hour. Here is the sequence, so nothing on the day is a surprise.

  1. Welcome and screening

    We sit down, go through your health questions, and talk about why you've come. If anything suggests hijama isn't right for you that day, we stop here — no charge for the conversation.

  2. Placement and dry cupping

    You settle on the table and cups are placed on the agreed points. Suction is applied gradually; most people describe it as a firm pulling, not pain. The cups rest for several minutes.

  3. The hijama itself

    Cups are lifted, light superficial scratches are made with a fresh sterile blade, and the cups are reapplied. A small amount of blood collects in the cups. You can ask for a pause at any point.

  4. Cleaning and dressing

    Cups come off, the skin is cleaned and disinfected again, and the points are dressed. The circular marks are normal and typically fade over days to a couple of weeks.

  5. Rest and aftercare

    You rest with water or tea before leaving — no rushing out the door. You take home written aftercare: keeping the area clean and covered, and taking it easy for the rest of the day.

Questions

Asked often, answered straight.

If your question isn't here, send it through the booking form — real questions shape this page.

Does hijama hurt?
The suction feels like a firm pull; the scratches are superficial and quick, and most people say the anticipation is worse than the sensation. You stay in control — say the word and we pause or stop.
Will hijama treat my condition?
We don't make that claim, and we'd encourage caution with anyone who does. Hijama is a traditional practice that many people value as part of their heritage and personal routine. For any medical condition, your doctor remains the right address — hijama is not a substitute for medical care.
What are the marks, and how long do they last?
The cups leave circular marks where blood was drawn toward the skin. They are expected, not a complication, and typically fade within a few days to two weeks depending on your skin.
Who should not have hijama?
Among others: people on blood thinners, people with bleeding or clotting disorders, pregnant women, and anyone with broken or infected skin at the intended sites. The screening conversation exists to catch these — and when in doubt, we ask you to check with your physician first.
Do you offer sessions for both men and women?
Yes — with gender-appropriate practitioner arrangements and full privacy. Details will be published with the booking calendar at opening.
How should I prepare?
Come lightly fed rather than fasting or stuffed, hydrated, and in loose clothing. Avoid strenuous exercise just before, and plan a calm rest of your day afterward.
